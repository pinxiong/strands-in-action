Ocean tides are the regular, periodic rise and fall of sea levels caused primarily by the gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun. The Moon's gravitational pull is the dominant force, exerting a stronger influence on the Earth's oceans due to its proximity. As the Moon orbits the Earth, its gravitational pull creates a bulge of water on the side of the Earth closest to the Moon, resulting in high tide. Simultaneously, an inertial bulge of water forms on the opposite side, creating another high tide due to the centrifugal force generated by the Earth-Moon system's rotation.

The areas between these bulges experience low tides. As the Earth rotates, different parts of the globe pass through these bulges, experiencing alternating high and low tides roughly every 12 hours and 25 minutes. The Sun also plays a role; during full and new moons, its gravitational pull aligns with the Moon's, causing higher high tides and lower low tides, known as spring tides. Conversely, during the first and third quarters of the moon, the solar and lunar gravitational forces are perpendicular, resulting in lower high tides and higher low tides, called neap tides.
